# Research dossier 01 — phytochemistry: identity, traditional record, and flavonoid chemistry of *Scutellaria baicalensis* Georgi

Every factual sentence carries an inline footnote backed by a machine-verified verbatim quote recorded in `research/findings_phytochemistry.json` (25 claims, 34 sources); the fetched text of every source is saved under `research/phytochemistry/pages/`. ## 1. Overview

*Scutellaria baicalensis* Georgi is a perennial Lamiaceae herb whose dried root is a famous traditional Chinese medicine [Growth years and post-harvest processing methods have critical roles on the contents of medicinal active ingredients of Scutellaria baicalensis. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33162677/, accessed 2026-11-14]; the binomial was validly published in 1775 with the Chinese name huang qin (黄芩) [Scutellaria baicalensis in Flora of China — https://www.efloras.org/florataxon.aspx?flora_id=2&taxon_id=200020285, accessed 2026-11-14], and Kew's Plants of the World Online accepts the species (native range: Siberia to Russian Far East and Vietnam) [Scutellaria baicalensis Georgi | Plants of the World Online | Kew Science — https://powo.science.kew.org/taxon/458155-1, accessed 2026-11-14]. The root's defining chemistry is root-specific 4'-deoxyflavones: more than 30 flavone types, including baicalin, baicalein, wogonin, wogonoside and oroxylin A [Scutellaria baicalensis, the golden herb from the garden of Chinese medicinal plants. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27730005/, accessed 2026-11-14]. Documented use spans 2000+ years, first recorded in the Shennong Ben Cao Jing around 200 AD [A comprehensive review on phytochemistry, pharmacology, and flavonoid biosynthesis of Scutellaria baicalensis. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31070530/, accessed 2026-11-14]; the Chinese Pharmacopoeia requires baicalin no less than 9% (2015 edition) [A comprehensive review on phytochemistry, pharmacology, and flavonoid biosynthesis of Scutellaria baicalensis. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31070530/, accessed 2026-11-14]. Two cautions frame the project: American skullcap (*S. lateriflora*, aerial parts) is a different species with a different organ-specific flavonoid distribution [Comparison of the major flavonoid content of S. baicalensis, S. lateriflora, and their commercial products. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18404307/, accessed 2026-11-14], and "skullcap" products have a documented history of adulteration with hepatotoxic germander (*Teucrium*) [Detection of the potential adulterant Teucrium chamaedrys in Scutellaria baicalensis raw material and extract by high-performance thin-layer chromatography. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19610368/, accessed 2026-11-14].

## 2. Evidence in depth

### 2.1 Botanical identity and the American skullcap problem

A botanical review confirms the root as the medicinal part and distribution across China, Russia, Mongolia, North Korea and Japan [Scutellaria baicalensis Georgi. (Lamiaceae): a review of its traditional uses, botany, phytochemistry, pharmacology and toxicology. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31236960/, accessed 2026-11-14]. The organ distinction is chemical: *S. baicalensis*'s four major root flavonoids are absent from its aerial parts, while *S. lateriflora* aerial parts contain more of them than its root [Comparison of the major flavonoid content of S. baicalensis, S. lateriflora, and their commercial products. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18404307/, accessed 2026-11-14]; baicalin localizes to roots of *S. baicalensis* but leaves of *S. lateriflora* [Exploring native Scutellaria species provides insight into differential accumulation of flavones with medicinal properties. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35915209/, accessed 2026-11-14]. American skullcap is nonetheless flavonoid-rich, its extracts predominantly baicalin, lateriflorin, dihydrobaicalin and baicalein [Analysis of Scutellaria lateriflora and its adulterants Teucrium canadense and Teucrium chamaedrys by LC-UV/MS, TLC, and digital photomicroscopy. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12852558/, accessed 2026-11-14] [Phytochemical and biological analysis of skullcap (Scutellaria lateriflora L.): a medicinal plant with anxiolytic properties. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14692724/, accessed 2026-11-14]. In Japan, roots of both species are pharmaceutical raw materials, aerial parts non-pharmaceuticals, and dried *S. baicalensis* root is registered in the Japanese Pharmacopoeia XV [Comparison of the major flavonoid content of S. baicalensis, S. lateriflora, and their commercial products. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18404307/, accessed 2026-11-14]. "Skullcap" hepatotoxicity history largely reflects germander: *Teucrium chamaedrys* is a reported hepatotoxic adulterant of American skullcap, HPTLC-detectable in Chinese skullcap extract [Detection of the potential adulterant Teucrium chamaedrys in Scutellaria baicalensis raw material and extract by high-performance thin-layer chromatography. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19610368/, accessed 2026-11-14], and comparative skullcap-germander phenolic profiling exists for that reason [Comparison of the phenolic component profiles of skullcap (Scutellaria lateriflora) and germander (Teucrium canadense and T. chamaedrys), a potentially hepatotoxic adulterant. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19402188/, accessed 2026-11-14].

### 2.2 Traditional record

The root first entered the materia medica in the Shennong Ben Cao Jing (Shennong's Classic of Materia Medica) around 200 AD [A comprehensive review on phytochemistry, pharmacology, and flavonoid biosynthesis of Scutellaria baicalensis. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31070530/, accessed 2026-11-14]. In TCM theory it is bitter and cold; it enters the lung, gallbladder, spleen, large intestine and small intestine meridians, clears heat and dries dampness, purges fire and detoxifies, stops bleeding and stabilizes pregnancy [Preparation, immunological and pharmacological effects of flavonoids in Scutellariae radix: a review. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41684516/, accessed 2026-11-14]. Classical indications quoted for it include "treating all kinds of fever, jaundice, diarrhea, edema, and sores" [Scutellaria baicalensis and its flavonoids in the treatment of digestive system tumors. — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11625591/, accessed 2026-11-14]. Shanghan Zabing Lun prescriptions include Gegen Qinlian, Huangqin, Huanglian Ejiao and Banxia Xiexin decoctions [Recent advances in Scutellariae radix: A comprehensive review on ethnobotanical uses, processing, phytochemistry, pharmacological effects, quality control and influence factors of biosynthesis. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39262990/, accessed 2026-11-14]. Huang-Qin is an important ingredient of Xiaochai Hutang (Xiao Chai Hu Tang; Japanese Sho-saiko-to), first described in the Shanghan Lun (On Cold Damage) by Zhang Zhongjing around 200 AD, its seven-herb composition including Bupleurum [Scutellaria baicalensis, the golden herb from the garden of Chinese medicinal plants. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27730005/, accessed 2026-11-14] [Role of B-lymphocytes in the immunopharmacological effects of a traditional Chinese medicine, xiao-chai-hu-tang (shosaiko-to). — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7682201/, accessed 2026-11-14]. The root is officially recognized in the Chinese, European and British pharmacopoeias [Preparation, immunological and pharmacological effects of flavonoids in Scutellariae radix: a review. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41684516/, accessed 2026-11-14]. Two millennia of continuous, enormous population exposure is the informal safety record the parent invokes; formal toxicology belongs to the safety stream.

### 2.3 The defining 4-deoxyflavones

Six flavones are the major bioactives of Radix Scutellariae: aglycones (baicalein, wogonin, oroxylin A) and their glycosides (baicalin, wogonoside, oroxylin A-7-glucuronide), all pharmacologically active [Pharmacological effects and pharmacokinetics properties of Radix Scutellariae and its bioactive flavones. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21928297/, accessed 2026-11-14]; baicalin is baicalein-7-O-glucuronide (C21H18O11) [Scutellaria baicalensis and its flavonoids in the treatment of digestive system tumors. — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11625591/, accessed 2026-11-14]. Recorded from root and hairy root are skullcapflavone I (5,2'-dihydroxy-7,8-dimethoxyflavone) and skullcapflavone II (5,6'-dihydroxy-6,7,8,2'-tetramethoxyflavone) [Applications, phytochemistry, pharmacological effects, pharmacokinetics, toxicity of Scutellaria baicalensis Georgi. and its probably potential therapeutic effects on COVID-19: a review. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32994803/, accessed 2026-11-14]. Scutellarin and its aglycone scutellarein are among the eight flavonoids quantified in roots, with baicalin, wogonoside, baicalein, wogonin, apigenin and chrysin [Growth years and post-harvest processing methods have critical roles on the contents of medicinal active ingredients of Scutellaria baicalensis. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33162677/, accessed 2026-11-14]. Root-specific accumulation reflects evolutionary divergence of the flavonoid pathway in the genus [Exploring native Scutellaria species provides insight into differential accumulation of flavones with medicinal properties. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35915209/, accessed 2026-11-14].

### 2.4 Content, distribution, and preparation

The Chinese Pharmacopoeia requires Huang-Qin baicalin ≥9% (2015 edition) [A comprehensive review on phytochemistry, pharmacology, and flavonoid biosynthesis of Scutellaria baicalensis. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31070530/, accessed 2026-11-14]; comparative assays run JP ≥10.0%, KP ≥10.0% total (baicalin+baicalein+wogonin), EP ≥9.0%, USP ≥11.0%/≥3.50%, WHO ≥9.0%, ISO ≥8.0% [Analysis on the current quality standards of Chinese materia Medica used in COVID-19 prevention and treatment. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32653649/, accessed 2026-11-14]. Content is regionally patterned: across 38 samples from seven main cultivated areas, baicalin, wogonoside and baicalein dominated and differed markedly by region [Comparative Study of the Flavonoid Content in Radix Scutellaria from Different Cultivation Areas in China. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36820244/, accessed 2026-11-14], and multidimensional indicators (flavonoids, physicochemical parameters, chromaticity, bioactivity) discriminate origin [Origin Identification of Scutellariae radix Based on Multidimensional Quality Indicators and Machine Learning Algorithms. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41752457/, accessed 2026-11-14]. Growth year and post-harvest handling drive composition: optimum harvest 2–3 years, root cortex richer than stele, drying or cutting-drying most preserving [Growth years and post-harvest processing methods have critical roles on the contents of medicinal active ingredients of Scutellaria baicalensis. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33162677/, accessed 2026-11-14]. Wine-processing (pao zhi) changes compound dissolution by altering pore structure [Anti-migraine effect of wine-processed Radix scutellariae: Pharmacodynamic verification in nitroglycerin-induced rats and correlation study between compounds dissolution and the fractal dimension. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32730879/, accessed 2026-11-14]; near-infrared spectroscopy quantifies baicalin, baicalein and wogonin in raw versus wine stir-fried root from seven regions [Rapid and Simultaneous Determination of Three Active Components in Raw and Processed Root Samples of Scutellaria baicalensis by Near-infrared Spectroscopy. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30005428/, accessed 2026-11-14]. Baicalin undergoes gastrointestinal hydrolysis with enterohepatic recycling [Pharmacokinetics and Bioavailability Enhancement of Baicalin: A Review. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30209794/, accessed 2026-11-14], and probiotic fermentation markedly modifies the bioactive components [Research progress on pharmacological properties and application of probiotics in the fermentation of Scutellaria baicalensis Georgi. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38903628/, accessed 2026-11-14].

### 2.5 Standardization, authentication, and contamination

Chromatographic fingerprints combined with quantitative multi-component analysis support quality evaluation [Quantitative analysis and chromatographic fingerprinting for the quality evaluation of Scutellaria baicalensis Georgi using capillary electrophoresis. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16989969/, accessed 2026-11-14]. Four variable chloroplast regions (matK-rps16, ndhC-trnV-UAC, psbE-petL, rps16-trnQ-UUG) differentiate *S. baicalensis* from adulterants, substitution with comparable species being a documented market risk [Comparative analysis of medicinal plants Scutellaria baicalensis and common adulterants based on chloroplast genome sequencing. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38191291/, accessed 2026-11-14]; whole chloroplast genomes act as super-barcodes for cultivated varieties indistinguishable morphologically before flowering [Identification of three cultivated varieties of Scutellaria baicalensis using the complete chloroplast genome as a super-barcode. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37019975/, accessed 2026-11-14]. Commercial extracts vary widely: of 10 analyzed, 4 contained no baicalin, with more than 11-fold potency variation [Quality evaluation of commercial extracts of Scutellaria baicalensis. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15489215/, accessed 2026-11-14]. Arsenic accumulation in *S. baicalensis* has been studied at plant level [Arsenic accumulation in Scutellaria baicalensis Georgi and its effects on plant growth and pharmaceutical components. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19577841/, accessed 2026-11-14]; heavy-metal accumulation in circulating TCM materials is a recognized problem [Spotlight on the accumulation of heavy metals in Traditional Chinese medicines: A holistic view of pollution status, removal strategies and prospect. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39244068/, accessed 2026-11-14], and pesticide-residue detection for herbal medicines is an urgent analytical need [Pesticide residue detection technology for herbal medicine: current status, challenges, and prospects. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38367162/, accessed 2026-11-14]. Notably, ChP 2015 sets no heavy-metal or pesticide tests for Scutellariae Radix (it sets sulfur dioxide ≤150 ppm), whereas JP, KP, EP, USP, WHO and ISO do [Analysis on the current quality standards of Chinese materia Medica used in COVID-19 prevention and treatment. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32653649/, accessed 2026-11-14].

## 3. Human evidence

Human data in this scope are randomized trials of combination products only. Eighty-five healthy adults taking 240 mg/day of a *S. baicalensis* stem extract plus *Acacia catechu* for four weeks showed time-related cognitive gains without advantage over placebo and good tolerability [Effects of Acacia catechu and Scutellaria baicalensis extract on cognitive function in a healthy adult population: a double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trial. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39701593/, accessed 2026-11-14]; fifty adults aged 40–80 showed greater post-influenza-vaccination increases in total IgA and IgG with the same species pair [Role of Acacia catechu and Scutellaria baicalensis in Enhancing Immune Function Following Influenza Vaccination of Healthy Adults: A Randomized, Triple-Blind, Placebo-Controlled Clinical Trial. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36413261/, accessed 2026-11-14]. No monopreparation trial was captured, so single-herb efficacy claims cannot rest on these results. Translation is shaped by baicalin's hydrolysis- and enterohepatic-recycling pharmacokinetics [Pharmacokinetics and Bioavailability Enhancement of Baicalin: A Review. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30209794/, accessed 2026-11-14]. One safety-relevant human signal for the safety stream: Japanese kampo labeling attributes interstitial pneumonia and drug-induced hepatopathy to scutellaria root, with baicalin historically considered the cause [Comparison of the major flavonoid content of S. baicalensis, S. lateriflora, and their commercial products. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18404307/, accessed 2026-11-14].

## 4. What is NOT established

- **SNBCJ grade classification** (upper/middle/lower grade) for huangqin: not verifiable in any fetched source despite targeted searching.
- **Traditional daily dose** (grams per day of dried-root decoction): not found in fetched sources; the Chinese Pharmacopoeia monograph was not directly accessible.
- **Field baicalin percentage ranges of dry root**: only pharmacopoeial minima are verified. One 2024 review instead states the Chinese Pharmacopoeia sets baicalin "not less than 8%" [Scutellaria baicalensis and its flavonoids in the treatment of digestive system tumors. — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11625591/, accessed 2026-11-14] — an internal inconsistency, since two fetched sources support ≥9% [A comprehensive review on phytochemistry, pharmacology, and flavonoid biosynthesis of Scutellaria baicalensis. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31070530/, accessed 2026-11-14] [Analysis on the current quality standards of Chinese materia Medica used in COVID-19 prevention and treatment. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32653649/, accessed 2026-11-14].
- **Steaming effects on composition**: not documented in fetched sources (verified preparation effects: wine stir-frying, drying/cutting-drying, fermentation, post-ingestion hydrolysis).
- **"Little baicalin" in American skullcap**: contradicted — *S. lateriflora* aerial parts contain more of the four flavonoids than its root [Comparison of the major flavonoid content of S. baicalensis, S. lateriflora, and their commercial products. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18404307/, accessed 2026-11-14], baicalin localizes to its leaves [Exploring native Scutellaria species provides insight into differential accumulation of flavones with medicinal properties. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35915209/, accessed 2026-11-14], and its extracts are predominantly baicalin [Analysis of Scutellaria lateriflora and its adulterants Teucrium canadense and Teucrium chamaedrys by LC-UV/MS, TLC, and digital photomicroscopy. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12852558/, accessed 2026-11-14].
- **Dating inconsistencies**: the SNBCJ first record is ~200 AD [A comprehensive review on phytochemistry, pharmacology, and flavonoid biosynthesis of Scutellaria baicalensis. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31070530/, accessed 2026-11-14] and 200–250 AD [Scutellaria baicalensis, the golden herb from the garden of Chinese medicinal plants. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27730005/, accessed 2026-11-14] in two reviews but "2nd century B.C." in another [Scutellaria baicalensis and its flavonoids in the treatment of digestive system tumors. — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11625591/, accessed 2026-11-14]; Georgi's 1775 publication is cited as "Reich. 1: 223" by Flora of China [Scutellaria baicalensis in Flora of China — https://www.efloras.org/florataxon.aspx?flora_id=2&taxon_id=200020285, accessed 2026-11-14] and "Reich 2: 223" by POWO [Scutellaria baicalensis Georgi | Plants of the World Online | Kew Science — https://powo.science.kew.org/taxon/458155-1, accessed 2026-11-14].
