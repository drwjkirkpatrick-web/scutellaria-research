# Dossier 06 — Safety, Toxicology, Drug Interactions, and Quality of *Scutellaria baicalensis*

Safety-stream dossier for *S. baicalensis as a Longevity Botanical Medicine* (W. J. Kirkpatrick, ND), compiled 2026-11-14. Every source below was fetched and archived as text under `research/safety/pages/`; structured claims with verbatim quotes are in `research/findings_safety.json`. Recent work (2019-2026) is weighted; older studies are kept as anchors.

## 1. Overview

The safety literature on this species conflates four separable questions: (1) liver injury attributed to **flavocoxid (Limbrel)**, a concentrated baicalin-catechin pharmaceutical blend; (2) historical "skullcap" hepatotoxicity, largely tied to **American skullcap** (*S. lateriflora*) and **germander (*Teucrium*) adulteration**; (3) whether authenticated *S. baicalensis* root itself injures the liver; and (4) interaction potential and quality variables. The synthesized picture: wide preclinical safety margins (26-week GLP rat NOAEL 2,000 mg/kg/day for a root extract), a rare idiosyncratic DILI signal concentrated in extracts, blends, and above-pharmacopoeial doses, interaction mechanisms documented mainly in vitro and in rodents (only two direct human studies), and quality caveats (adulteration, aflatoxin, heavy metals) rather than intrinsic toxicity.

## 2. Hepatotoxicity evidence

**Flavocoxid (Limbrel).** Flavocoxid pairs baicalin (from *S. baicalensis*) with catechins (from *Acacia catechu*), standardized above 90% purity, prescribed at 500 mg twice daily for osteoarthritis. The DILIN prospective study attributed 4 of 877 enrolled DILI cases to it - all women, onset averaging 11.2 weeks, mean peak ALT 1,268 U/L, all recovering without liver failure after cessation (Chalasani et al. 2012 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22711078/, accessed 2026-11-14). LiverTox records premarketing aminotransferase elevations in up to 10% of patients but above 3x ULN in only 1-2%; clinically apparent cases with onset 1-5 months, hepatocellular or mixed pattern, moderate severity, no reported liver failure or death, resolution in 1-3 months; likelihood score C (LiverTox: Flavocoxid — https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK548129/, accessed 2026-11-14). FDA received 194 adverse event reports for Limbrel products (2007-2017) identifying "a close relationship" with "drug-induced liver injury (DILI), pancreatitis, and hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP)," and requested a nationwide recall on December 18, 2017 (FDA recall letter — https://www.fda.gov/media/109965/download, accessed 2026-11-14); the manufacturer's announcement records 30 reports of elevated liver function tests or acute HP, no deaths, full resolution after discontinuation, and distribution ceased December 21, 2017 (Primus recall announcement — https://www.fda.gov/safety/recalls-market-withdrawals-safety-alerts/primus-announces-voluntary-nationwide-recall-all-lots-within-expiry-prescription-medical-food, accessed 2026-11-14). Population context: among 3,337 new flavocoxid users versus 6,674 matched NSAID users, hospitalized liver injury ran 3.2 versus 2.4 per 1,000 person-years (aHR 1.16, 95% CI 0.23-6.01) - rare and statistically indistinguishable (Curtis et al. 2020 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32305527/, accessed 2026-11-14).

**American skullcap and germander.** LiverTox states skullcap "has been implicated in rare instances of clinically apparent liver injury," but "in most cases multiple herbal medications were being taken," and "in some instances phytochemical analysis has identified significant adulterants (germander) or mislabeling" (LiverTox: Skullcap — https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK548757/, accessed 2026-11-14). Analytical studies document *Teucrium* adulteration of commercial skullcap material: LC-UV/MS and microscopy distinguish *S. lateriflora* from *T. canadense* and *T. chamaedrys* by phenylpropanoid markers (teucrioside) absent from Scutellaria (Gafner et al. 2003 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12852558/, accessed 2026-11-14); HPTLC detects *T. chamaedrys* in *S. baicalensis* raw material and Univestin extract down to 0.5% (w/w) (Hong et al. 2009 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19610368/, accessed 2026-11-14); hyperspectral methods were proposed because "due to intentional or unintentional adulteration, cases of hepatotoxicity have been reported" (Sandasi et al. 2014 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25184892/, accessed 2026-11-14). The classic hepatotoxic "skullcap" record is therefore substantially confounded by species substitution and adulteration rather than by authentic Scutellaria.

**Authenticated *S. baicalensis*.** A small genuine case literature exists: two Naranjo-probable (score 6) hepatotoxicity cases from Chinese skullcap within Move Free Advanced, whose Uniflex extract pairs Chinese skullcap with black catechu as flavocoxid does (Linnebur et al. 2010 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20586134/, accessed 2026-11-14); a case with inadvertent positive rechallenge (Yang et al. 2012 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22855699/, accessed 2026-11-14); a 51-year-old man with jaundice 10 days after starting a *Scutellariae baicalensis* 400 mg supplement (bilirubin rising to 24.0 mg/dL), resolving within 12 weeks (Braude & Bassily 2019 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30957370/, accessed 2026-11-14); and recurrent DILI on incidental readministration of two Scutellariae Radix-containing Kampo formulas (Shimada et al. 2018 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29434136/, accessed 2026-11-14). A 2026 multicenter Shanghai study estimated confirmed SBG-DILI at 0.095% (72/75,564 outpatients), with risk significantly increased above the 10 g/day pharmacopoeia recommendation (adjusted OR 2.210) and ABCB1 rs2032582 C/C overrepresented in cases (58.3% vs 16.3% of controls) (Fu et al. 2026 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42665171/, accessed 2026-11-14), replicated in a 2025 case report (Tang et al. 2025 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41431018/, accessed 2026-11-14). A 2024 editorial over a 936-case HILI meta-analysis describes skullcap-HILI as typically hepatocellular and mild-to-moderate, with 14.2% mortality among published cases - explicitly tempered by publication bias (Soldera 2024 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38495279/, accessed 2026-11-14).

Counterweights: 17 patients took 1,335 mg dried root daily for a mean 444 days with no significant change in ALT, AST, alkaline phosphatase, or bilirubin and no clinical hepatic dysfunction; the authors noted every prior published case involved at least one co-supplement with an established hepatotoxicity association (Puri et al. 2019 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31557595/, accessed 2026-11-14), and LiverTox's annotation of that series states "there is no convincing evidence that it causes liver injury" (LiverTox: Skullcap — https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK548757/, accessed 2026-11-14). Preclinically the root is hepatoprotective in challenge models: baicalin 300 mg/kg after acetaminophen prevented hepatic necrosis and cut mortality from 43% to 0% in mice (Jang et al. 2003 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14686800/, accessed 2026-11-14), and baicalin inhibited aflatoxin-B1-induced apoptosis in human L02 hepatocytes (Wen et al. 2024 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38913091/, accessed 2026-11-14). A GLP-compliant 26-week rat study of root extract found no mortality, no target organ of toxicity, NOEL 1,000 and NOAEL 2,000 mg/kg/day, and an acute minimum lethal dose above 5,000 mg/kg (Song & Kim 2026 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42931707/, accessed 2026-11-14); baicalin's own subacute NOAEL is 2,000 mg/kg, with reversible hepatorenal changes only at 4,000 mg/kg (Yang et al. 2025 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40918531/, accessed 2026-11-14). Two caveats stay open: the GLP paper cites a literature review identifying 16 suspected *S. baicalensis*-associated DILI cases (Song & Kim 2026 full text — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13709419/, accessed 2026-11-14), and a 2018 rat study of a Huangqin ethanol extract reported potential chronic liver toxicity, but its PubMed record (32186063) returns a mismatched abstract, leaving it unverified.

## 3. Interactions and contraindications

**Transporters.** The cleanest human evidence is hepatic uptake: baicalin 50 mg three times daily for 14 days reduced rosuvastatin AUC(0-72) by 47.0% in OATP1B1*1b/*1b volunteers, attenuating across haplotypes - a loss-of-efficacy, genotype-dependent interaction (Fan et al. 2008 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17851565/, accessed 2026-11-14). For cyclosporine, the single human study (500 mg baicalin once) found no clinically significant effect and good tolerability (Liu et al. 2019 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31156436/, accessed 2026-11-14), but rodent data are bidirectional: Scutellaria decoction (1-2 g/kg) cut oral cyclosporine Cmax 62.9-79.6% and AUC 55.2-82.0%, with coadministration advised against (Lai et al. 2004 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14994190/, accessed 2026-11-14), while multi-dose baicalin lowered oral ciclosporin bioavailability 25-38% via intestinal absorption inhibition and P-glycoprotein induction, prompting a recommendation for dosage adjustment and blood-level monitoring with long-term co-use (Tian et al. 2019 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30663770/, accessed 2026-11-14).

**CYP450.** In vitro, baicalin inhibits CYP1A2 (mixed-type Ki 25.4 uM) with large interindividual variability unrelated to genotype (Gao et al. 2014 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24587011/, accessed 2026-11-14); baicalein and wogonin competitively inhibit CYP2C9-mediated diclofenac 4'-hydroxylation (Ki <= 2.2 uM), a plausible basis for warfarin-type interactions (Si et al. 2009 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19074529/, accessed 2026-11-14); baicalein inhibits CYP3A4 (IC50 12.03 uM) and tripled simvastatin AUC in rats, with dose adjustment advised (Meng et al. 2021 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34214011/, accessed 2026-11-14). Reviews conclude the bioactives modulate absorption, distribution, metabolism, and elimination of many drugs via efflux pumps and CYPs in both beneficial and adverse directions (Zhou et al. 2021 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33711551/, accessed 2026-11-14), complicated by microbiota-driven baicalin-baicalein interconversion (Noh et al. 2016 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26978333/, accessed 2026-11-14).

**Pharmacodynamic cautions.** All three major flavonoids are antithrombotic: baicalin inhibited thrombin-catalyzed fibrin polymerization, platelet function, and thrombin/FXa activities and prolonged in vivo bleeding time (Lee et al. 2015 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24849036/, accessed 2026-11-14); baicalein inhibited agonist- and tumor-cell-induced platelet aggregation (Kim et al. 2014 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25268843/, accessed 2026-11-14); wogonin/wogonoside showed anticoagulant effects in mice (Ku & Bae 2014 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25020199/, accessed 2026-11-14) - supporting a bleeding-additivity caution with antithrombotic drugs, though no clinical case reports exist. Wogonin inhibits MAO-A (IC50 6.35 uM) and MAO-B (20.8 uM) in vitro, with baicalein moderately MAO-A-selective (Lee et al. 2017 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28109809/, accessed 2026-11-14); baicalein decreased brain MAO-B in MPTP mice (Song et al. 2021 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34666128/, accessed 2026-11-14) and suppressed brain monoamine oxidase activity in chronically stressed mice (Tang et al. 2026 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42976909/, accessed 2026-11-14) - relevant to serotonergic co-medication, though no clinical MAO-inhibitor-type interactions are documented. Baicalein (with taxifolin) significantly reduced blood glucose in alloxan-diabetic rats, warranting hypoglycemic-additivity awareness (Liang et al. 2026 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42170474/, accessed 2026-11-14).

## 4. Special populations, quality, and dosing context

**Pregnancy, lactation, pediatrics.** Huangqin has millennia of traditional pregnancy use - "to clear heat, protect the fetus, and avoid a miscarriage" - but modern pregnancy-related literature is limited to disease models (recurrent spontaneous abortion, preeclampsia, preterm birth, fetal growth restriction, gestational diabetes) (Fang et al. 2023 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36216728/, accessed 2026-11-14). Developmental studies show a modest, reversible signal: dose-dependent fetal skeletal variations (lumbar rib) and ureter dilation in rats at roughly 12.5-25 g/kg water-extract concentrate without maternal toxicity (Kim et al. 1993 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8448419/, accessed 2026-11-14); the lumbar rib resolves postnatally and was judged a transient fetal variation at 186 mg/kg/day, a 100-fold increase over typical human intake (Ko et al. 2010 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20549833/, accessed 2026-11-14); mice showed no external or skeletal malformations up to 32 g/kg/day, though the top dose raised maternal liver and kidney weights (Tian et al. 2009 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19283861/, accessed 2026-11-14). No human pregnancy, lactation, or pediatric safety data were located.

**Contamination and quality.** In a UPLC-MS/MS survey of 386 Chinese Pharmacopoeia herbal medicines, pyrrolizidine alkaloids were detected in 271 samples (0.1-25,567.4 ug/kg), 20 exceeded the 0.007 ug/kg bw/day baseline, and 10 had lifetime MOE values below 10,000; *Scutellaria barbata* was positive (total PAs 61.7 ug/kg; EDI 0.026 ug/kg bw/day at the 30 g/day pharmacopoeial maximum; lifetime MOE 8,958; two-weeks-per-year MOE 232,918), and the only species the authors flagged as potential risks were *Arnebia euchroma*, *Tussilago farfara*, *Eupatorium fortunei*, and *Eupatorium lindleyanum* - *S. baicalensis* is not named in the publication (Wang et al. 2021 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8000276/, accessed 2026-11-14). Heavy metals: among four medicinal species including *S. baicalensis* sampled in Hebei, China, some Cu, Cd, Hg, and Pb exceedances occurred, but the comprehensive chronic health-risk assessment "showed that none of the four medicinal herbs can create a health risk" (Meng et al. 2022 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34075510/, accessed 2026-11-14). Aflatoxin B1 was detectable in 2 of 6 commercial *S. baicalensis* samples, all below national safety limits (Liu et al. 2025 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41003537/, accessed 2026-11-14). Given the documented *Teucrium* adulteration of skullcap raw materials, verified botanical authentication and contaminant testing are the operative quality safeguards.

**Dosing context.** The Chinese Pharmacopoeia raw-material dose is 3-10 g/day; the rat NOAEL corresponds to roughly 75 g/day of raw Scutellariae Radix for a 60-kg adult - about 7.5-25 times the pharmacopoeial dose - and the Shanghai pharmacovigilance data place the DILI risk gradient above 10 g/day (Song & Kim 2026 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13709419/, accessed 2026-11-14; Fu et al. 2026 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42665171/, accessed 2026-11-14). Human exposure anchors: 1,335 mg dried root daily for about 15 months (Puri et al. 2019 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31557595/, accessed 2026-11-14); flavocoxid 500 mg twice daily (LiverTox: Flavocoxid — https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK548129/, accessed 2026-11-14); and isolated baicalein dosed at 100-2,800 mg single-dose in 72 healthy adults (Li et al. 2014 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25219601/, accessed 2026-11-14) and 200-800 mg multiple-dose without serious accumulation (Pang et al. 2016 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27352310/, accessed 2026-11-14), with later single-dose (100-800 mg) and multiple-dose (200-600 mg) trials reporting only mild adverse events and good tolerability (Dong et al. 2021 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33753147/, accessed 2026-11-14; Li et al. 2021 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34156161/, accessed 2026-11-14).

## 5. What is NOT established

- Warfarin interaction case reports: none located; CYP2C9 inhibition by baicalein/wogonin remains a mechanistic caution only.
- Statin interaction case reports: none located; the sole human evidence is the controlled rosuvastatin study (which showed reduced, not increased, statin exposure).
- Human lactation data and pediatric safety data: none located.
- Clinical MAO-inhibitor-type interactions (e.g., tyramine/hypertensive) for baicalein or wogonin: none documented.
- Pyrrolizidine alkaloid contamination of *S. baicalensis* products specifically: not found; PubMed "Scutellaria AND pyrrolizidine" yields no primary survey, and the documented PA-positive Scutellaria species is *S. barbata*.
- The "16 suspected *S. baicalensis* DILI cases" figure is cited secondhand within the 2026 GLP paper; the primary review was not independently retrieved.
- PMID 32186063 (chronic liver toxicity of a Huangqin ethanol extract in rats) returns a mismatched PubMed abstract; unverified.
- Regulatory action specific to *S. baicalensis* root (distinct from Limbrel/flavocoxid): none located in US or EU sources.

## 6. Key sources

- Chalasani et al. 2012 (PMID 22711078) - DILIN flavocoxid case series; anchor for supplement-associated DILI.
- LiverTox: Flavocoxid (NBK548129) and Skullcap (NBK548757) - regulatory-grade monographs with the germander-adulteration caveat.
- FDA requested-recall letter (December 18, 2017) and Primus recall announcement - the regulatory record (194 adverse event reports; 30 hepatic/pulmonary reports; no deaths).
- Fu et al. 2026 (PMID 42665171) - first population incidence estimate for SBG-DILI (0.095%), dose dependence above 10 g/day, ABCB1 rs2032582 pharmacogenetics.
- Song & Kim 2026 (PMID 42931707; PMC13709419) - GLP 26-week NOAEL and the NOAEL-to-pharmacopoeial-dose margin.
- Fan et al. 2008 (PMID 17851565) and Liu et al. 2019 (PMID 31156436) - the two direct human interaction studies (OATP1B1/rosuvastatin; cyclosporine).
- Wang et al. 2021 (PMID 33809536) - 386-herb PA survey containing the *S. barbata* row.
- Puri et al. 2019 (PMID 31557595) - longest human root-supplementation liver-function series.
- Soldera 2024 (PMID 38495279) - 936-case HILI meta-analysis mortality context, with publication-bias caveat.
- Zhao et al. 2019 (PMID 31236960) and Bunchorntavakul & Reddy 2013 (PMID 23121117) - species-level toxicology and HDS-hepatotoxicity anchors.

## 7. Open questions for the parent

- Framing rarity: weigh the rare idiosyncratic signal (DILIN 4/877; IR 3.2/1,000 person-years; Chinese outpatient incidence 0.095%) against hepatoprotection data and the GLP NOAEL. Suggested framing: "rare, idiosyncratic, largely extract/blend- and suprapharmacopoeial-dose-associated," with baseline and periodic LFTs for long-term users.
- Dose language: anchor to the 3-10 g/day decoction range, 1,335 mg root, flavocoxid 500 mg twice daily, and baicalein 100-2,800 mg phase I exposure.
- Whether ABCB1 rs2032582 pharmacogenetics (2025 case report + 2026 cohort) merits its own paragraph or a footnote.
- Interaction counseling depth: rosuvastatin efficacy loss (OATP1B1), cyclosporine monitoring, CYP2C9/warfarin theoretical-only, antiplatelet additivity - all mechanistic with almost no clinical event reports.
- Species discipline: keep *S. lateriflora* (American skullcap) and the germander-adulteration history separate from *S. baicalensis*; the PA finding belongs to *S. barbata*, not *S. baicalensis*.
- Chronic-dosing gap: human long-term safety rests on one 444-day uncontrolled series plus traditional exposure; flag this as the key evidence gap for chronic-supplementation (longevity) claims.
